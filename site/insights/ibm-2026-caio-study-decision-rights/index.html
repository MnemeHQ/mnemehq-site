IBM's 2026 CAIO Study: Accountability Without Decision Rights

IBM surveyed 1,667 chief AI officers (CAIOs) and equivalent leaders and found that only 18% have full control over AI investment decisions, while 61% say AI decision rights are clearly defined and consistently applied. Where coding agents write production code, the same gap reaches the architecture: who may change a decision, and what shows that an agent followed it.

By Theo Valmis·December 2026

What IBM’s 2026 chief AI officer study found

On November 16, 2026, the IBM Institute for Business Value (IBV), IBM’s research arm, published A template for high-impact AI, its 2026 study of chief AI officers (CAIOs). IBV and Oxford Economics, an economic research firm, surveyed 1,667 executives responsible for enterprise AI strategy, governance and delivery between April and June 2026, across 33 geographies and 20 industries. The study counts chief data and AI officers, heads of AI and similar roles as CAIOs too.

Start with the budget. Only 18% of respondents say they have full control over AI investment decisions, and the report adds that just 15% have full authority over AI workforce strategy. IBM’s announcement quotes the foreword by Joanne Wright, IBM’s senior vice president for transformation and operations: the outcomes CAIOs are accountable for “often depend on decisions made by others.”

Four other findings matter to engineering leaders:

  • Agents already act. 48% of CAIOs say AI agents start and carry out actions across workflows. 75% say governance, risk or compliance delays AI-driven action.
  • Oversight is defined more often than decision rights. 73% have defined the decisions where human oversight is non-negotiable. Only 61% say AI decision rights, meaning who decides what, are clearly defined and consistently applied. IBM puts the two side by side under the heading “CAIOs can accelerate AI by clarifying accountability.”
  • Strong performers can stop things. IBM scores each organization on an AI success index built from three reported results: the share of AI initiatives with measurable business outcomes, the share that reach production, and the time to scale a success. It calls the CAIOs who practise the eight behaviors most linked to a high score “high-impact CAIOs.” They are nearly four times as likely to say they can quickly pause, adjust or redirect AI systems without disrupting operations.
  • They also scale faster. The high-impact group scales a successful initiative across the enterprise in eight months. Everyone else takes 13.5 months. 96% of the group stop or cut funding for initiatives that fail to deliver, even with senior sponsors, against 46% of other CAIOs.

These are answers to one survey, and the success index uses the results respondents report, so read the gaps as associations. IBM’s own methodology describes behaviors “associated” with success, not proven causes.

Where the gap shows up in software delivery

IBM writes about AI across the whole business, and its finding is about budgets and workforce plans. Its study does not discuss software architecture. Applying the finding to a company whose coding agents write production code is our extension, not IBM’s argument.

The CAIO answers for what AI delivers. Some of what AI now delivers is code. Every change a coding agent makes either respects or breaks the architectural decisions the engineering team has made: which service may read customer records, which libraries are allowed, where a module boundary sits. Many teams record these as architecture decision records (ADRs), short documents in the repository that state a decision and the reason for it. Others keep them in agent instruction files, the text files a coding agent reads at the start of each session, such as CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md.

The CAIO does not own those decisions and should not. Engineering does. But when an agent breaks one, the cost lands on an outcome the CAIO reports. That is the same shape as IBM’s budget finding: the CAIO answers for the result, and the decision rights sit with the people who own the architecture. Unless engineering records which decisions each change was checked against, the CAIO has no way to see whether the agents followed those decisions.

Why adoption metrics do not answer the question

Most AI dashboards report adoption: seats in use, the share of code an assistant wrote, pull requests merged per week, agents in production. They show that agents are busy, not whether the architecture held.

The failure they miss is architectural drift: code moving away from the decisions it was meant to follow, one reasonable-looking change at a time. The damage shows up months later, when a boundary has eroded and the fix is a rewrite. On an executive dashboard, a team with rising drift and a team without it show the same adoption chart. Delivery metrics have the same blind spot, including the DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) measures of speed and stability.

Separate implementation autonomy from decision rights

IBM’s 73% and 61% measure two different things. Deciding where a human must step in does not settle who owns each decision. For coding agents, we split permission in two:

  • Implementation autonomy: the agent may choose how to write a change, within the decisions that apply to it. This is the part that should grow.
  • Decision rights: the right to change or waive an architectural decision. It belongs to a named owner, through a review that records who approved what. An agent never holds it, however good its output.

IBM’s high-impact CAIOs say they can pause or redirect AI quickly. The engineering version is a check that can stop one change that breaks a decision, before it merges, without halting the agent or the team. With that kind of stop in place, a team can widen what agents do on their own and keep its decisions intact.

The engineering decision governance concept page covers the full model: which decisions govern a change, who may change them, and what evidence shows they were applied.

What executives can ask engineering to measure

None of these numbers come from IBM or from Mneme. They are things an engineering team can count in its own repositories, and each answers a question a CAIO is already accountable for:

  • Written decisions. How many architectural decisions are recorded, and how many are current rather than replaced by later ones.
  • Checked decisions. How many of those a machine check can fail. A decision no check can fail is guidance, not control.
  • Stopped changes. How many agent changes a check blocked or flagged in a month, and against which decisions. A sudden rise points to a decision the agents keep missing.
  • Exceptions. How many waivers were granted, by whom, and how long they stayed open.
  • Reconstruction. For a merged change picked at random, whether the team can show which version of each decision it was checked against and what the check returned.

The last one is the test IBM’s findings point to. Reconstruction shows which decision a change was checked against. Pair it with the exceptions count, which shows who approved each waiver, and the CAIO can see whether the people holding decision rights were the ones who used them.

Start with one repository

Skip the company-wide program. Pick one repository where agents already write a lot of code, and do five things:

  1. List the architectural decisions it already has, in ADRs, agent instruction files and team documents.
  2. Name an owner for each one, and require that owner’s review on any change to the decision file. On GitHub, a CODEOWNERS file names the owner of given files, and a branch protection rule that requires code-owner review makes that owner’s approval mandatory on any pull request that touches them.
  3. Turn the two or three decisions that matter most into checks that run before an agent writes a file and again in continuous integration (CI), the automated checks on every pull request.
  4. Record each verdict with the decision it came from.
  5. After a month, report the five measures above to the CAIO, next to the adoption numbers.

That gives the CAIO evidence from one codebase instead of a policy statement for all of them.

Where Mneme fits

Mneme covers steps 3 and 4 above and part of step 1. It does not touch AI budgets.

Mneme keeps decisions in the Decision Index, a versioned record of the repository’s decisions stored in a file inside the repository. Only accepted decisions are active, and an active decision is enforced only when it carries a typed rule, a precise condition a program can check. The rule that hooks and CI enforce today is an exact piece of text that must not appear in the files the rule covers, such as a banned library name.

Claude Code is Anthropic’s coding agent, which runs in a developer’s terminal. Mneme’s hook for it is a small program Claude Code runs before each file edit, and it blocks an edit that breaks such a rule. A single quoted cat heredoc, a shell command that writes a file in one step, is also blocked before the write. Most other files written through shell commands are checked at the end of the agent’s turn instead, after the write. In CI, the GitHub Actions gate fails a pull request whose changes break such a rule. The JSON verdict from mneme check --json names each decision whose rule it evaluated, and which version, including when the change passes.

The hook fails open by default. That means if the check itself cannot run, for example because it crashed or took too long, the hook lets the edit through rather than blocking it.

Mneme does not hold decision rights. The Decision Index records what each version of a decision said, not who approved it, so approvals belong in code-owner review: a CODEOWNERS entry for the decision files, with branch protection that requires the owner’s approval on any change to them. The Architecture Protection Audit lists the decisions a repository documents and shows which ones a Mneme check protects, which makes it a quick way to do step 1. Mneme does not make an organization compliant with anything, and IBM’s study does not mention it.
